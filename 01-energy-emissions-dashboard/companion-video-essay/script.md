# The Myth of Energy Transitions: A Historical Perspective

## Introduction

Hello I'm Kai. Today, I want to challenge a widely accepted idea: the so-called "energy transition." Conventional wisdom suggests that societies move from one energy source to another, phasing out the old in favor of the new. However, as historian Jean-Baptiste Fressoz argues, this is not how history has unfolded. Instead of transitions, we see accumulation—new energy sources are added without replacing the old. The data supports this claim, and I will use two graphs to illustrate this point.

A modern parallel to this can be seen in the production of renewable energy infrastructure. Fossil fuels are deeply embedded in their supply chains. The manufacturing of solar panels relies on coal-powered industrial processes, particularly for refining silicon. Likewise, wind turbines require large amounts of steel, which is primarily produced using coal in blast furnaces. Just as wood remained crucial for coal mining, coal itself remains essential for producing the very technologies we hope will replace it.

## The Reality of Energy Accumulation

The first graph shows global primary energy consumption by source over time. If the transition model were correct, we would expect to see energy sources being replaced—coal substituting biomass, oil replacing coal, and renewables taking over from fossil fuels. But the reality is quite different. The graph reveals that instead of substitution, each new energy source has been added on top of the previous ones, leading to an overall increase in consumption. This is what Fressoz refers to as "symbiotic expansion"—where technological progress fuels more, not less, energy use.

## Energy Growth: A Self-Perpetuating Cycle

The second graph, showing the proportion of global energy consumption by source, further reinforces this idea. Despite the rise of renewables, fossil fuels still dominate, and their absolute consumption continues to grow. This contradicts the optimistic notion that green energy will simply replace fossil fuels. Instead, our energy use expands with each new technological advancement. Historically, even when coal became dominant, societies still used massive amounts of wood—often to support coal mining itself.

## Conclusion: What This Means for the Future

If history teaches us anything, it is that energy transitions do not happen naturally. If we want real change, we must rethink our approach. Instead of assuming that technology alone will solve our problems, we must focus on sufficiency—reducing material and energy consumption. Without this shift in mindset, we risk repeating the same cycle: adding more energy sources while still burning coal, oil, and gas at record levels. The challenge ahead is not just technological but cultural and economic.
